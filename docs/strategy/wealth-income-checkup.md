# Wealth and income checkup

The checkup is a repeatable review that turns a household's data into a short list of strengths, gaps and next actions. It is not a score to chase and it is not advice.

## Inputs

- 12 months of completed cash-flow rows.
- Current account balances and card commitments.
- Budget period and planned items.
- Assets, liabilities and contributions.
- Pension, provident and study-fund products.
- Insurance policies and premiums.
- Income streams and their stability.
- Household composition, life stage and explicit preferences.

## Seven checks

### 1. Cash buffer

How many months of essential spending can the household cover with liquid money?

- Use essential spending, not total spending.
- Subtract reserved cash and near-term commitments.
- Show the assumption and the confidence.
- A negative buffer is an urgent finding; a high buffer is not automatically "too much".

### 2. Cash-flow resilience

What happens if income stops for one month, or arrives late?

- Compare committed outflows with liquid cash.
- Include card bills, loan payments, rent or mortgage, insurance and utilities.
- Flag concentration in a single income source.

### 3. Debt cost

What is the weighted cost of debt, and which balance is most expensive?

- Separate mortgage, consumer loans, overdraft and card instalments.
- Show the rate and the remaining term where known.
- Do not recommend refinancing without the fee and the remaining term.

### 4. Savings rate and direction

What share of income is retained, and is it going to long-term savings?

- Compare income, spending and contributions over 12 months.
- Separate emergency savings from retirement savings.
- Treat irregular income carefully; a good month is not a trend.

### 5. Pension and long-term products

Is there a long-term product, what does it cost, and is the contribution consistent?

- Show the balance, the monthly deposit and the fee on deposits and balance.
- Flag missing products and stale data.
- Do not project a pension without stated return and inflation assumptions.

### 6. Protection

Which risks are covered, duplicated or missing?

- Map policies to life, health, disability, home, car and mortgage-life needs.
- Show renewals and premiums.
- A gap is a question to investigate, not a product to buy.

### 7. Rights and obligations

Are there time-limited rights, credits or filings the household should know about?

- Use a reviewed knowledge entry with a year and a source.
- Explain eligibility at a high level and link to the official process.
- Do not calculate a final entitlement.

## Output

The checkup produces:

1. three strengths, with evidence;
2. three gaps, ordered by urgency and confidence;
3. one next action per gap, with a deep link;
4. the assumptions and as-of dates;
5. a "what would change this" note for each gap.

## Life-stage adjustments

The same numbers mean different things at different stages. The life-stage mode changes the emphasis, not the arithmetic:

- **Student:** cash buffer, income stability, small emergency fund, first pension product.
- **Early career:** savings rate, pension fees, insurance basics, debt cost.
- **Family:** buffer, protection, childcare and housing costs, education goals.
- **Business owner:** separated business cash, tax reserve, income concentration, pension contributions.
- **Pre-retirement:** withdrawal assumptions, pension fees, sequence risk, health cover.
- **Retired:** income sources, liquidity, renewals, estate documents.

## Failure modes

- Treating a missing product as a zero.
- Treating a one-off expense as a trend.
- Using the calendar month when the household uses a salary day.
- Showing a projected return without a range or an assumption.
- Confusing "no data" with "no risk".
